So we trained a sign detector on our boxes: detector plus this CNN reads whole seals at 41 percent sign error without even knowing the count. |
| 4 | 1:00-1:38 | Compositor: crops fly into a bank, stone is painted clean, signs are pasted along a real text; composites vs real; result bars | Professor Ashwin suggested we build new inscriptions out of the real signs we have. We cut every hand-boxed sign out of the training seals, painted the signs out of real seal surfaces, and pasted real signs along real corpus texts. Every composite comes with its exact sequence and boxes, for free. We made five thousand and trained a reader that needs no cutting, a CRNN with CTC loss. On a site it never saw, Harappa, sign error fell from 58 to 53 percent, and it got the sign count right on 65 seals instead of 53. |
| 5 | 1:38-2:00 | Jar sign bars; masked-prediction demo | Milestones two and three: the jar sign ends 35 percent of texts and almost never starts one, and the order is more predictable read right to left. Hide a sign, and bigram statistics recover it 35 percent of the time, 60 in the top five. A small transformer did worse: with under two thousand short texts, counting beats learning. |
| 6 | 2:00-2:24 | Guardrail bars | Milestone four: a Naive Bayes classifier tells real texts from shuffled ones 81 percent of the time. But Mesopotamian deity symbols, which are not writing, pass too. And a Markov chain with no language reproduces Zipf's law and the jar sign. Only groups of three signs set the Indus texts apart. |
| 7 | 2:24-2:44 | Error-propagation chart | Are our readings good enough for these statistics? With random mistakes, the structure washes out below about 60 percent accuracy, and our old 31 percent reader was not enough. Our new hand-box CNN, at 74 percent and with its own real mistakes, keeps every signal: real texts still beat shuffled ones 74 percent of the time. So the structure we measure is the script's, not the reader's. |
